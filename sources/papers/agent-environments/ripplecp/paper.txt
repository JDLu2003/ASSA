                                                                                     Abstract

                                                 Agent checkpoint systems decide what state is recovery-relevant, how to snapshot it,
                                                 and whether rollback is admissible. None decides which of the safe boundaries they
                                                 expose are worth materializing. We formulate this as counterfactual checkpoint
                                                 advantage, the reduction in future recovery cost obtained by checkpointing a
                                                 candidate rather than skipping it, and measure it by driving a CP branch and a
                                                 S KIP branch to the same logical failure and recovering both under matched model,
                                                 tool, verifier, and stopping conditions. On a frozen pilot of 12 SWE-bench Verified
                                                 tasks and 106 real recovery branches, checkpointing saves 49.4 s per task, and
                                                 that figure resolves into two regimes two orders of magnitude apart. The first
                                                 checkpoint returns 100.0 s on 156.5 s of protected work, a conversion of 0.64; a
                                                 second one step later returns −1.1 s on 41.8 s, a conversion of −0.03. Recovery is a
                                                 re-derivation rather than a replay, so preserved work is a poor guide to saved work,
                                                 and the classical elapsed-work rule misprices the second checkpoint by its full
                                                 nominal cost. We identify where placement can pay, and set the bar a placement
                                                 policy must clear.



                                        1   Introduction

                                        Long-horizon agents increasingly behave like stateful systems: they alternate model calls with shell
                                        commands, file edits, tests, external tools, retries, and pauses. A late crash can force substantial
                                        re-execution, and a growing set of runtimes makes checkpoint and restore for such agents practical.
                                        C RAB detects recovery-relevant OS effects [1], D ELTA B OX targets low-latency sandbox rollback [2],
                                        and DART reasons about semantically admissible recovery points [3]. Each of them answers where a
                                        checkpoint may be taken. None answers where one should be: among the safe boundaries a runtime
                                        exposes, which are worth paying to make durable?
                                        Classical placement answers that by trading checkpoint overhead against expected recomputation [4],
                                        which presumes recomputation cost is known. For agents it is not. Downloading a gigabyte may
                                        be cheap to repeat, while rediscovering a one-line dependency incompatibility can determine every
                                        subsequent edit, build, and test. We expect checkpoint-worthy agent state to be sparse in bytes but
                                        dense in downstream influence (Appendix I), which would make the usual proxies of elapsed work
                                        and bytes changed unreliable. Whether it does is an empirical question, and Section 4 answers it
                                        directly. Either way the quantity is one to measure rather than assume.
                                        R IPPLE CP makes it measurable. Rather than labelling a state “important,” we run two matched
                                        futures from the same candidate: checkpoint now, or skip now. Both are driven by the real agent to
                                        the same logical failure and recovered under the same model, tool, verifier, and stopping conditions,
                                        so their cost difference, the checkpoint advantage, comes from executed system behaviour rather than
                                        from an LLM judge or a human annotation. This paper reports that measurement on a frozen pilot;
                                        learning a policy from it is future work.
The result reshapes the placement question: the aggregate advantage is large, but it decomposes
almost entirely into the value of having a checkpoint at all rather than of choosing where to put it.
We contribute the advantage formulation, a paired-recovery harness producing labels from executed
agent branches, and the pilot that separates those regimes and prices each.

2   Checkpoint advantage formulation
Let an agent produce states s0 , . . . , sT . At safe candidate st , the placement action is at ∈
{CP, SKIP}. Let c− denote the latest materialized checkpoint before t, and let f ∼ F denote
a future failure. We define a cost-to-go under action a,
                                                                          
                       J(st , a) = Ef ∼F Cplace (a) + L Recover(st , a, f ) ,                  (1)
where Cplace (SKIP) = 0, Cplace (CP) = Cckpt (st ), and L is future recovery/re-execution loss.
For connection to value-based decision making, define Q(st , a) = −J(st , a). The checkpoint
advantage is
                               ACP (st ) = Q(st , CP) − Q(st , SKIP)                                (2)
                                         = J(st , SKIP) − J(st , CP).                               (3)
Thus ACP (st ) > 0 means checkpointing saves more future cost than it adds. Our primary loss is
wall-clock redo time; repeated tokens, tool calls, and task success remain separate metrics rather than
hidden scalar weights.
The formulation is independent of the snapshot mechanism: R IPPLE CP assumes candidates already
satisfy the runtime’s admissibility rules and asks only whether a safe opportunity is economically
worth materializing. It is thus complementary to semantic recoverability [3], resume conformance [5],
rollback defenses [6], and semantic-environment isolation [7].

3   Measuring checkpoint advantage
Recovery tracers. Offline, we choose a candidate st and a later failure f . We execute two matched
worlds: CP, which materializes st and recovers from it after f ; and S KIP, which recovers from c− .
Their measured cost difference estimates ACP (st ). Both branches restore into isolated workspaces,
run the same pinned agent configuration against the same logical failure step, and stop under the
same rule: success on the task’s hidden-test verifier, or a predeclared recovery budget. Branch order
is randomized per pair. Because the agent runtime exposes no controllable sampling seed, repetition
rather than seed matching controls model nondeterminism; the recorded pair seed governs branch
order and scheduling only. The label is thus produced by executed system behavior, with no LLM
judge and no human annotation; Figure 1 in Appendix B diagrams the construction.

Cost accounting. The primary cost J is wall-clock recovery time plus measured checkpoint creation
time, so CP is charged its own overhead and a positive advantage is a net saving. Repeated tokens
and tool calls, checkpoint bytes, restore latency, and task correctness are recorded separately, never
folded into J.

From labels to placement (proposed, not evaluated here). The labels are meant to train a
placement policy from features cheap to compute online, such as execution cost, state size, verifier
progress, and provenance-graph statistics, with a gradient-boosted regressor [8] predicting A
                                                                                            bCP (st )
under a budget rule; Appendices C–F specify it. This paper measures ACP only, and Section 4 reports
how much room such a policy would have.

What the design contrasts. Taking candidates in trajectory order fixes two things at once, and
they must be read apart. In every early cell the candidate is the first safe boundary, so S KIP restores
the pristine workspace and the contrast is one checkpoint versus none. In every late cell an earlier
checkpoint exists, so S KIP restores a mid-task state one step back, giving one additional checkpoint
versus the one already taken. Redo distance is identical in both cases, one step for CP and two for
S KIP, so the strata are separated by fallback identity rather than distance.

The advantage is real, and it belongs to one regime. Pooled over both strata, checkpointing
saves 49.4 s per task (95% task-bootstrap CI 34.1–65.5), net of the 4.41 s creation cost charged to
CP. The decomposition in Table 1 places almost all of it on the first checkpoint, worth 100.0 s (95%
CI 72.4–131.1) and 388,812 re-executed tokens. A second checkpoint one step later returns −1.1 s
(95% CI −9.5–8.4), an interval that straddles zero and is smaller in magnitude than the cost of taking
it. CP cost is nearly identical across strata, 100.8 s against 100.6 s, while the late stratum preserves
strictly more progress. The regimes differ by two orders of magnitude, so the decomposition rather
than the pooled mean is what to design against.

Preserved work is a poor guide to saved work. A checkpoint at candidate step k protects exactly
step k’s nominal work, which makes the conversion from protected to saved work directly measurable.
The first checkpoint protects 156.5 s and returns 100.0 s, a conversion of 0.64. The second protects
41.8 s and returns −1.1 s, a conversion of −0.03. Work concentration accounts for part of the gap,
since these trajectories place 73.9% of nominal work in step 1, but not for the collapse in conversion.
An elapsed-work rule [4] assumes conversion near one and prices the second checkpoint at its full
41.8 s. Recovery is a re-derivation rather than a replay, and only a paired measurement separates the
two.

Cheap proxies rank inside a regime, not across it. Ordinary work proxies rank measured ad-
vantage well among the first-checkpoint cells and not at all among the additional-checkpoint cells
(Table 2). Ordering candidates is therefore the easy part, and the signal a policy needs is the regime
boundary itself. Two rows are worth naming. Bytes changed ranks at +0.70, so checkpoint value
here is not sparse in bytes and the sparse-but-influential state our intuition describes does not arise in
SWE-bench issue resolution. The importance score the agent assigns each step of its own accord,
without being asked to price recovery, ranks −0.58 and never positively in any subset, which is why
labels come from executed behaviour rather than the model’s judgement. The null is regime-specific:
conditioned on the late failure alone the later boundary is cheaper on 5 of 12 tasks.

5   Related work
Agent checkpoint and restore is moving quickly: C RAB on semantics-aware checkpoint/restore [1],
D ELTA B OX on low-latency sandbox C/R [2], DART on admissible local recovery [3], plus resume
contracts [5], rollback safety [6], causal progress [10], and semantic isolation [7]. R IPPLE CP claims
no new mechanism; it asks what a safe opportunity those systems expose is economically worth.

That question has a long lineage outside agents. Adjoint checkpointing places a bounded number of
snapshots along a computation to trade storage against recomputation [11], activation checkpointing
makes the same trade inside a training step [12], materialized view selection persists intermediate
results under a space budget [13], and classical interval placement balances overhead against expected
redo [4]. In all of these, recomputation cost is deterministic and analytically known, so placement
reduces to optimization. For a stochastic agent it is neither. Recovery is a re-derivation rather than a
replay, so its cost must be measured, and Section 4 shows the measured cost and the analytic proxy
differing by the whole quantity.
Causal Agent Replay [14] intervenes on real LLM-agent trajectories to attribute failures to individual
steps, and meets the same obstacle we do: under a stochastic policy, re-executing a trajectory perturbs
everything downstream of the intervention. It resolves this with a point-of-commitment rule over
resampled rollouts, whereas we use repeated paired branches, randomized order, and a predeclared
sign-stability criterion. The questions differ, one asking which step caused a failure and the other
what a checkpoint there is worth, but the instrumentation problem is shared and neither yields to
replaying recorded costs.


6    Discussion and limitations

Three things follow for a runtime builder. Take one checkpoint at the first safe boundary: it captures
the available value for 4.41 s. Do not spend budget ranking later boundaries until the workload is
known to have more than one expensive step. Price a candidate by the recovery it avoids rather than
the work it preserves. The bar for a learned policy is now concrete: beat the first-boundary rule at a
matched budget on a workload where conversion varies across candidates.
That bar is not clearable here, which is the main limitation. Trajectories are 3–4 steps (mean 3.25)
with 73.9% of the work in the first, so the workload is close to a single expensive step followed by
verification. Re-cutting the same tasks at tool-call boundaries would yield 29.2 candidates per task,
24.8 inside that first step, though the logs carry no per-item timings and so need re-collection rather
than re-analysis (Appendix G). Workloads with heterogeneous accumulated state, such as dependency
resolution or multi-stage builds, are where the hypothesis lives.
Three constraints bound the claims. The first safe boundary is always the one whose S KIP world
restarts from scratch, so “first” and “earliest” are not separable. The additional-checkpoint estimate
sits at per-cell resolution, 15.0 s of noise against a 14.4 s effect, so the task-level bootstrap rather than
any single cell supports it. And 12 tasks from three repositories in one tier, one agent, one model
version is a small effective sample. We report no learned predictor and no matched-budget study;
Appendices D and F specify both.


7    Conclusion

Checkpoint placement for agents is a question about value, not mechanism. Checkpointing saves
49.4 s per task pooled, but 100.0 s of that belongs to the first checkpoint and a second one step later
returns −1.1 s on 41.8 s of protected work. The quantity that governs placement is the conversion
from preserved work to saved work, which this harness measures and elapsed-work proxies assume.

A    Detailed experimental protocol
Task selection. Task IDs were frozen before any paired run. The workload is 12 tasks from SWE-
bench Verified, the human-validated subset of SWE-bench [9]: four each from django/django 4.2,
sympy/sympy 1.12, and scikit-learn/scikit-learn 1.3, all drawn from the benchmark’s
15 min–1 hour difficulty tier. Exact issue IDs, base commits, benchmark source checksum, hidden-test
patches, normalized verifier commands, and pinned Python 3.10 environments are retained with the
run artifacts. Base-fail/gold-pass validation succeeded for all 12 tasks and all 24 expected worlds;
this establishes benchmark integrity only and is not an agent result. Tasks that never reach a safe
candidate would be excluded and reported; none were. Because this study measures ACP rather
than fitting a predictor, no train/validation/test split was drawn, and uncertainty is a task-level boot-
strap throughout. A predictor study would additionally require repository-level separation, ideally
leave-one-repository-out.

Trajectory collection. One nominal trajectory per task was recorded with Codex CLI 0.147.0-
alpha.6.5, GPT-5.4, xhigh reasoning at sandbox level workspace-write and approval policy never,
capturing every model call and observable tool boundary with timestamps, token counts (input, cached
input, output), tool arguments and return status, workspace hashes, verifier outcomes, and produced
artifacts. Raw JSONL event streams, stderr, prompts, verifier outputs, and per-branch timings are
retained beneath the run directory. The agent runtime does not expose a controllable model-sampling
seed; the recorded pair seed therefore controls branch order and experiment scheduling, not model
randomness. This is a limitation of the harness, not a design choice, and is the reason repetitions
rather than seed matching are used to control nondeterminism.

Candidate boundaries. Candidates come only from safe boundaries exposed by the runtime:
post-tool-return, post-commit, and post-test points. The placement model never decides checkpoint
correctness. From 27 nominal safe boundaries we selected 24 cells under a frozen stratification: one
early and one late checkpoint boundary per task, followed by the next logical failure step. Candidates
therefore sit at steps 1–3 and failures at steps 2–4. Selection was completed before any paired
recovery was executed. Because candidates are taken in trajectory order, the first selected boundary is
also the only one whose S KIP world has no earlier checkpoint to fall back on: the early cells restore
the pristine workspace and the late cells restore a mid-task state one logical step back. Boundary
position and fallback identity are therefore coupled by construction, which is why Section 4 reports
the strata as two marginal contrasts rather than as an comparison of early against late. This realizes
the temporal axis of the protocol only. The protocol also called for stratifying over physical size,
semantic progress, and ripple score, and for including late as well as near failure offsets; at this scale
we spent the cell budget on the timing contrast and on repetition instead. One consequence is that the
pilot cannot test the paper’s motivating “sparse in bytes, dense in downstream influence” hypothesis,
which needs the size and ripple axes.

Paired execution and stopping rule. Each cell was run with two repetitions by default. A separate,
resumable stabilization pass then froze the cells whose first two paired advantages changed sign
and ran exactly one additional pair for each, adding 5 pairs and preserving the predeclared 2-or-3
repetition rule without broadening expense. Branch order was randomized per pair (26 CP-first, 27
S KIP-first across 53 pairs). Both branches of a pair share the same task state, logical failure step,
model and tool settings, and stopping rule. A branch runs the agent to its own completion under
a hard per-branch cap of 1800 s, after which the task’s hidden-test verifier gates success. The cap
was never reached, since the longest branch spent 463 s of agent time, so in practice every branch
terminated on agent completion and was then judged by the verifier. Branches on the two tasks whose
nominal trajectory never passed the verifier therefore terminate with a failing verdict rather than at a
cost ceiling, which is why those tasks enter the analysis only as a termination-cost sensitivity.

Trustworthy and adaptive foundation-model systems. More broadly, reliable agent execution
depends not only on recovering state, but also on controlling what models retain, adapt, expose,
and act upon: recent work studies selective unlearning across LLMs, vision models, and embod-
ied agents [15–17], together with deployment constraints for embodied foundation models at the
edge [18]. Complementary directions address privacy leakage [19], fairness and retrieval-guided
debiasing [20, 21], personalized adaptation [22], and model explainability [23], highlighting that
trustworthy behavior depends on preserving or modifying the right internal and external information

Checkpoint contents. A checkpoint is archive-backed logical checkpoints containing workspace
files and preserved agent-visible state. Concretely, the backend archives the task workspace, excluding
.git, virtualenvs, and build/cache directories, together with the preserved transcript and discovered
facts, and records creation latency and compressed size for every artifact. Checkpoint creation latency
was 4.38 s mean (median 4.09 s; range 2.50–6.68 s) over the 53 CP branches; restore latency was
9.69 s mean (median 6.76 s; p95 20.8 s) over all 106 branches; compressed checkpoint size was mean
13.94 MB; median 10.44 MB; range 7.25–23.35 MB over the 39 stored artifacts. Process state and
tool-session state are not captured; the harness reconstructs agent-visible state from the archived
transcript rather than from an OS-level image.

B    Counterfactual advantage oracle
For each trajectory, candidate st , and sampled future failure fj :
1. restore to st from a common pre-candidate state;
2. in the CP branch, materialize st ; in S KIP, do not;
3. drive both branches to the same logical failure location and inject a fail-stop termination;
4. recover CP from st and S KIP from c− under matched model, tool, and verifier settings, in
   randomized branch order;
5. continue to the task’s verified endpoint or a fixed recovery budget, applying the same stopping
   rule to both worlds;
6. record total redo time, repeated tokens and tool calls, task correctness under the hidden-test verifier,
   and measured C/R overhead.

In the pilot of Section 4, K = 1: each selected candidate carries a single frozen logical failure, and

Edges must be observable: command→file-write, file-version→test-result, build-input→build-output,
tool-result→artifact, or explicit workflow dependencies. If any natural-language dependency is
inferred by an LLM, it should be isolated as a separate feature family and ablated. Calling these
dependency or ripple features is a claim about observable provenance, not about causal influence; the
counterfactual recovery measurement, not the graph, is the ground-truth target.

D     Small-data training protocol (future work)
No predictor is trained in this paper. This appendix records the protocol a follow-on study should use,
so that the measurement design and the eventual learning design can be reviewed together.
The project assumes no pre-existing checkpoint-value dataset; labels come from the counterfactual
oracle of Appendix B. Statistical independence is at the task/repository level, so candidate states must
never be split randomly: all states from one task belong to one partition, and leave-one-repository-out
is preferable once enough repositories are available. The pilot reported here yields 24 labelled cells
from 12 tasks, which is enough to estimate the population effect but not enough to fit and honestly
evaluate a predictor; scaling the labelled set is the precondition for the rest of this appendix.

Primary model. Start with linear/ridge regression and gradient-boosted trees. The target is mea-
sured ACP (st ); an alternative binary target is ⊮[ACP > 0], which the pilot suggests is a meaningfully
unbalanced problem (17 of 24 cells positive). Tune only on validation tasks. Report rank quality
alongside error, because placement is a prioritization problem.

Required baseline. Any predictor must be compared against the rule implied by the measured
decomposition, namely take the first checkpoint then stop, at a matched budget. The pilot measures
the decomposition; it does not run such a policy. Given how sharply advantage separates by fallback
identity (Section 4), a learned model that does not beat that rule has not demonstrated that learning is
necessary, and the correct conclusion would be to report the negative result and keep the simpler rule.

Why not deep RL first? Online agent episodes are expensive and the action space is only {check-
point, skip}. The supervised advantage label already estimates the local action difference directly.
Deep Q-learning is justified only if sequential interaction among checkpoint decisions materially
improves end-to-end cost; Appendix E states that hypothesis separately.

E     Optional sequential Q-learning extension (future work)
Checkpoint placement is sequential because a checkpoint changes the identity of the next previous
checkpoint and consumes budget. To test whether this interaction matters, define simulator state

redo/recovery cost when a simulated failure occurs. A fitted-Q iteration (FQI) baseline [25] would
learn Q(z, a) from trace-replay transitions rather than from expensive new online agent episodes.

Decision criterion. Q-R IPPLE should enter the main paper only if it provides a meaningful held-out
benefit over local advantage ranking at the same budget. If the gain is negligible, the negative result
should be reported and the simpler model kept. This prevents “RL for RL’s sake.” Neither Q-R IPPLE
nor the local ranking policy it would be compared against has been run on the real workload, so this
paper reports no result for either.

A caution from the pilot. Trace-replay transitions are only as good as the cost model behind them.
The additional-checkpoint contrast shows a mean advantage of −1.1 s, smaller in magnitude than
the 4.41 s cost of taking the checkpoint, so a simulator that models redo cost as monotone in elapsed
work would systematically misprice exactly the decisions a budgeted policy has to get right. Any
fitted-Q study should therefore be validated against measured paired recoveries, not only against
replayed traces.

Three readings. Work proxies rank advantage well inside the first-checkpoint regime and not at

model’s judgement.

Why checkpoint archive size is not in that table. Archive size correlates −0.31 with advantage,
which invites reading as evidence that physical size does not predict value. It is not. Archive size
here is a repository fingerprint: within-repository spread is 1% for Django, 12% for scikit-learn, and
39% for SymPy, against 16.1 MB of overall spread. Correlating it with advantage largely tests which
repository a cell came from. A genuine size feature has to vary within a repository, which is what the
bytes a step modifies does.

G.4   Measurement reliability

Mean within-cell standard deviation of paired advantage across repetitions is 24.1 s. Split by stratum,
the first-checkpoint cells have 33.1 s of noise against a 121.9 s mean effect (noise-to-signal 0.27),
while the additional-checkpoint cells have 15.0 s against 14.4 s (noise-to-signal 1.04). The additional-
checkpoint effect is therefore at the resolution of a single cell measured two or three times, and the
aggregate claim rests on the task-level bootstrap rather than on any individual cell. A study wanting
to resolve an effect of that size per cell should budget roughly an order of magnitude more repetitions.
For planning, one label cost 8.4 min of agent time.

G.5   What a finer candidate set would cost

The frozen study takes candidates at coarse turn boundaries: one early and one late per task. The
nominal event logs already record every tool call, so the boundaries for a finer re-cut can be counted
without collecting anything new. Across the 12 nominal trajectories there are 350 completed tool-call
items (276 command executions, 34 file changes, the rest agent messages and planning items): 29.2
per task, of which 24.8 on average (range 12–41) fall inside the first turn, the one carrying 73.9% of
the nominal work, where this design currently places exactly one candidate.
That is the missing resolution, and it is available on the existing workload. What is not available is
the cost of those boundaries: the recorded events carry turn-level token and timing totals only, with
no per-item timestamps, so the work protected by a tool-call candidate cannot be priced from the
current artifacts. A re-cut therefore requires re-collecting nominal trajectories with per-item timing
instrumented, after which the paired-recovery harness applies unchanged. We flag this as the concrete
next step rather than an open-ended call for a deeper workload.
